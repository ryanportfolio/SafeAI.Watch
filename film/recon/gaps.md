## Hook candidates

Timelessness tag on each.

1. **"DeepSeek V4 scores better on DeepSeek's self-reported evaluations than on CAISI evaluations."** `durable`. A government tester finding a lab's own numbers higher than independent numbers. The pattern (check the check) holds for any future model, and it is not about Anthropic or a US lab.
2. **"at least one successful attack was found against all of the target frontier models"** (250,000 attempts, 13 models, CAISI/Gray Swan). `durable`. Plain, calm, applies to all labs at once. Pair with "differed sharply in how many".
3. **"task correctness alone is insufficient to assess agent safety"** (Singapore and Korea institutes). `durable`. A measurement principle, not a model score, from two non-US, non-UK bodies.
4. **Independent reviewers spent "six days" on premises at OpenAI and took no payment** (METR/Redwood). `durable`. An independent look at a lab's raw data is a lasting practice pattern; calm visual of two outsiders in a room. Caveat: OpenAI could redact non-public information.
5. **"Independent third-party testing by Apollo Research found Muse Spark has the highest rate of evaluation awareness they have observed to date."** `durable` as a disclosure practice (a lab printing an unflattering outside finding in its own report); the finding itself is `perishable`. Meta, not Anthropic.
6. **Gemini 3.7 Flash "is observant enough to correctly assess when it is in a testing environment, but it cannot successfully bypass testing restrictions."** `perishable`. Tied to one model version and likely to change. Use only if a capability-then-limit pairing is wanted.

Not recommended as hooks: FAR.AI cost numbers (`perishable`, model versions already rolled over once), FLI grades (`perishable`, one edition, panel judgment).

### Check 1: Altman, "None of these levels are remotely acceptable."

- **Primary source:** OpenAI, "Sam Altman's remarks at the United Nations Security Council", https://openai.com/index/sam-altman-un-security-council-remarks/ , dated September 23, 2026, labeled "Remarks as delivered". Fetched headless, HTTP 200.
- **Cross-check:** UN Transcripts, https://transcripts.un.org/en/sc/10228 , Security Council 10228th meeting, 23 September 2026. Its automatic transcript has the same sentence ("None of these levels are remotely acceptable."). Note the UN Transcripts page says it is speech recognition and "not official records".
- **Where it sits:** Altman says there are "two ways that AI progress could go very badly that we have to avoid." The quote closes his discussion of the first way, losing control of the future to AI. The second way, concentration of power, follows it.
- **Full surrounding passage (verbatim, one sentence omitted):**
  > First, we could lose control of the future to AI. The risk is that it moves so fast that people can no longer follow what's happening or intervene when needed. This would obviously be terrible.
  >
  > The industry must not accept too much technological risk just because the benefits are too great and that they feel too important to slow down. Beating companies in a competitive pace is not a reason to make rash decisions. Nor do we believe we are locked in a race where we are unable to do that. We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future. [one sentence omitted here; it is about not building systems where alignment, monitorability and safety guarantees cannot be made]
  >
  > We need to understand what these systems are doing and have strong evidence that they will do what people intend, even as they get very, very smart. Actually especially as they get very very smart. It doesn't matter whether people put the risk of catastrophe at 10%, or 1%, or 12%, or .1%. None of these levels are remotely acceptable. And we should not train models that we cannot make an extremely strong case that we will be able to keep under human control.
  >
  > So that's one way things can go wrong.
- **What "these levels" refers to:** the four probabilities of catastrophe that Altman lists in the sentence just before it: "10%, or 1%, or 12%, or .1%". He is saying the size of the catastrophe probability does not change his conclusion: no such level is acceptable, so models should not be trained unless there is an "extremely strong case" they can be kept under human control.
- **What it is not:** it is not an OpenAI estimate of catastrophe risk, and it does not say the risk is at any of those levels. The numbers are illustrative. The list is odd (12% sits between 1% and .1%); it is the same in OpenAI's text and in the UN automatic transcript, so do not "fix" it.
- **Conditions and speaker limits:** the claim is normative ("not remotely acceptable"), and OpenAI's pledge to slow ("We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future.") is a statement of intent. Altman runs a company that builds these systems. FLI's July panel counts OpenAI among labs that weakened pause pledges (see section C), which is the counterweight if the film shows the pledge.
- **Safe ways to use it:**
  - Full sentence with its lead-in: "It doesn't matter whether people put the risk of catastrophe at 10%, or 1%, or 12%, or .1%. None of these levels are remotely acceptable."
  - Or the short quote with on-screen attribution "on the risk of catastrophe, to the UN Security Council, 23 Sept 2026".
  - Do not run "None of these levels are remotely acceptable" alone with no reference to risk of catastrophe: "levels" then reads as capability, compute or current risk.
  - Speaker credit: "OpenAI CEO Sam Altman".
- **Corroborating in-repo row:** `warnings.md` already holds this quote with the 2026-09-23 date; this check confirms it and adds the full passage.
