# Harris voice study: slice `ai-dilemma`

Study of how Tristan Harris explains AI in "The A.I. Dilemma" (2023) and in later solo and near-solo talks. Voice study only: the factual claims inside these talks are his and were not checked here.

## Reading conventions (read first)

- **Speaker rule.** "The A.I. Dilemma" is a two-person talk (Harris and Aza Raskin) and the YouTube captions carry no speaker labels. Every line below is attributed from one of three evidence levels, marked in each row:
  - **[CHT-PDF]**: CHT's own labelled transcript of the podcast edition (Episode 65). Official, speaker-labelled. It covers about 60% of the talk and omits some passages.
  - **[GT-label]**: speaker label taken from the third-party transcript "Guerrilla Transcripts" (full talk, labelled). Not primary. I checked it against [CHT-PDF] wherever the two overlap (Oppenheimer, plane, three rules, Golem, combinatorial compounding, race to intimacy, Day After, China, closing) and they agree every time. Treat as strong but not official.
  - **[solo]**: a solo talk where Harris is the only speaker (video title and channel say so).
- **Caption quotes.** Quotes from YouTube auto-captions keep the captioned words. I cut filler ("um", "uh") and stutter repeats and added punctuation and capitalization; no other word changed. Odd caption spellings are left and flagged `[sic]`. Re-check against the audio before any on-site use.
- **Raskin lines** are labelled as Raskin. They show the shared voice but are not Harris.
- Lines already confirmed in `film/recon/harris.md` (TED 2025 and four CHT episodes) are cited by their row, not re-quoted at length.

## How he frames it

### 1. Benefit and danger in the same object: "these stories are all true", then "underneath"

His standard move is to say the benefit claims are true, in plain words and more than once, then turn to what sits under them. He never calls the benefits spin.

- Harris on the second contact with AI. **[CHT-PDF]** (video t=11:50, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xoVJKj8lcNQ):
  > "We're saying AI's going to make us more efficient, it's going to help us write things faster, write code faster, and solve impossible scientific challenges, solve climate change, and help us make a lot of money. And these things are all true. These are real benefits. These are real things that are going to happen. And also behind that, we've got people worried about, well, what about AI bias? What if it takes our jobs? We need transparency."
- Same move, solo, with a personal stake. Harris, Wisdom & AI Summit, t=8:32 **[solo]** (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bKCLLkYRYik):
  > "these stories are all true they're awesome stories. I want personalized tutoring, I want personalized medicine. I don't want anyone I love to be hit with a disease they can't get out of. But underneath these stories are these harms"
- Same move applied to social media, AI for Good 2024, t=4:53 **[solo]** (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F6XR0byERZY):
  > "these stories are true. These are totally things that social media has done. But underneath those stories we started to see beneath the iceberg there's some problems but these are symptoms"

### 2. The both-sides claim as "inseparable", shown by paired examples

He argues the good and bad uses cannot be pulled apart because they are one capability, and he proves it with a matched pair.

- Harris, AI for Good 2024, t=13:21 **[solo]** (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F6XR0byERZY):
  > "we want the promise of AI without the peril of AI. We want the benefits without the harms. And the challenge is, can the technology that knows how to make cool AI art about humans be separated from the same technology that can create deep fake child porn? They're part of the same image model. Can the technology that can give every kid in Africa a one-on-one biology tutor be separated from the AI model that can give every Isis terrorist a biological weapon tutor? They're inseparable. They're all part of the same model."
  (The first pair names child sexual abuse material. The tutor pair carries the point without it.)
- Harris, Wisdom & AI Summit, t=20:44 **[solo]** (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bKCLLkYRYik): the drug-discovery example, "I wanted to discover less toxic versions of these drug compounds ... then what they did is they took that paper and they flipped it around ... within 6 hours it found 40,000 toxic molecules including rediscovering VX nerve gas."
- Raskin, not Harris, gives the shortest version, in the CHT PDF only. Raskin **[CHT-PDF]**: "The very best thing is also the very worst thing, every time." It follows Harris's line: "You can identify how to optimize supply chains, you can also break supply chains. You can identify how to find new drugs to heal humanity, and you can also find things that can break humanity." (Harris, **[CHT-PDF]**.) Neither sentence appears in the March 9 video captions; they are in the podcast edition only.
- Later spoken form, Harris to Krista Tippett (2026-07-16): "a positive infinity of new benefits at the same time that it presents almost a negative infinity of risk in the same object." See `harris.md` row 1.

### 3. Danger can erase the benefit, so the benefit is not a defence

- Harris, closing the talk **[GT-label]**, video t=1:06:44, labelled Tristan; the passage itself opens "Just to repeat what Aza said", which also marks the speaker as the non-Aza host (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xoVJKj8lcNQ):
  > "AI will continue to also create medical discoveries we would never had. It's going to create new things that can eat, you know, microplastics and solve problems in our society. It will keep doing those things. And we are not wanting to take away from the fact that those things will happen. The problem is if as the ladder gets taller, the downsides of, hey, everybody has a bioweapon in their pocket, these are really really dangerous concerns. And those dangerous concerns undermine all the other benefits."
- Harris, AI for Good 2024, t=15:59 **[solo]**:
  > "no matter how high the skyscraper of benefits that AI assembles, if it can also be used to undermine the foundation of society upon which that skyscraper depends, it won't matter how many benefits there are."
- Raskin, same idea in the CHT intro **[CHT-PDF]**: "no matter how good your utopia you create, if your dystopia is bad enough, it doesn't matter."
- Same idea in 2026: "the upsides do not prevent the downsides, but the downsides can undermine and prevent the upsides from ongoingly existing" (`harris.md` row 4).

### 4. Camps and labels: he describes a stance, not a group, and uses the label on himself

- On being called a doomer, Harris, Wisdom & AI Summit, t=0:18 **[solo]** (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bKCLLkYRYik; caption text `[sic]` "I like Joan", a name or word garbled):
  > "I like Joan can sometimes be called a Doomer that focuses on only the downsides of how everything can go wrong. But I just want to say that all of this is about how do we get to the future that we want, right? That's what I want."
- He concedes the skeptic's view and says where he stood. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "we'll say that there's many reasons to be skeptical of AI. I have been skeptical of AI. Aza, maybe a little bit less so." And Harris, Wisdom, t=7:21 **[solo]**: "every concern that you sort of thought was overblown about AI up until 2017, I would have agreed with you completely."
- He asks for systems thinking over sides. Harris, Wisdom, t=27:47 and t=28:05 **[solo]**:
  > "it means being able to confront difficult truth ... it means focusing on changing the incentives, seeing things as a system rather than seeing things as good guys or bad guys ... instead of saying is X good or bad, synthesizing the polarities"
- He names the three emotional stances and picks the third. Harris, Presencing 2026, t=8:09 to 9:07 (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fTv2TsWyapc), crediting Daniel Schmachtenberger for the terms:
  > "pre-tragic optimism is essentially naive optimism. It's sort of reflexive, I want it to be good so let me just focus on the good ... basically optimism that's in denial of the reality. Then ... you can get caught in despair, nihilism, cynicism, the tragic ... And what we really need are people who grieve their way through the full metabolism and recognition of the meta-crisis that we are facing. And on the other side of that grief is the love you have for the world ... post-tragic optimism, which is coming from love to protect that world as best as possible."
- Camps as blind spots, from the 2025 and 2026 rows already confirmed: "those who want to focus on the benefits of open source don't want to think about the things that come from chaos. And those who want to think about the benefits of safety and regulated AI control don't want to think about dystopia." (TED 2025, `harris.md` row 9). And "you either talk about the good and your mind isn't really holding onto or thinking about the bad" (Tippett, `harris.md` row 3).

### 5. "Not ideological", "not anti-AI", "not about not building AI"

- Harris **[CHT-PDF]**, intro: "The important thing here is that we are not ideological about how the world should look. Ultimately what we care about is just, what will it take to get this right?"
- Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "This is not about not building AI. It's about, just like we do with drugs or with airplanes where you do not just build an airplane and then just not test it before you onboard people onto it." (Video t=58:51 has the near-identical "This is not about stopping the research. This is not about not building AI. It's about slowing down the public deployment.")
- Harris, Wisdom, t=22:47 **[solo]**: "I hope you don't hear I'm not some kind of all AI is bad thing. The same tech that I've showed you can also find breakthroughs in cancer ... I'm not denying a single one of those things. I want that future too. But the question is how are we getting there, what's the safe way to get there."

### 6. Fear and agency: warn, pause, name the feeling, then hand back choice

- He tells listeners to stop and breathe at the hardest point, in all three earlier talks. Harris, The A.I. Dilemma, t=51:12 **[GT-label]** (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xoVJKj8lcNQ):
  > "This is extremely difficult material and I just for a moment, just take a genuine breath ... there's this challenge when communicating about this, which is that I don't want to dump bad news on the world. I don't want to be talking about the darkest horror shows of the world. But the problem is if it's kind of a civilizational right of passage [sic] moment, where if you do not go in to see the space that's opened up by this new class of technology, we're not going to be able to avoid the dark sides that we don't want to happen."
  Then, t=52:28 **[GT-label]**: "I get that this seems impossible. And our job is to still try to do everything that we can." And t=52:48: "We can still choose which future that we want."
- Harris, AI for Good 2024, t=15:12 **[solo]**: "at this point in the presentation I would actually just encourage you, if you want to just take a breath together. We're all here because we care about which future we get ... we can still choose the future that we want, but we have to actually see the risk clearly so we know the kinds of choices that we need to make."
- Harris, Wisdom, t=0:01 and t=20:27 **[solo]**: opens "you want to take just a deep breath with me for a second", and mid-talk: "I'm not trying to scare. I just want to show you honestly the set of realities that are coming from the way that we're releasing this technology."
- He warns about the mind's reflex after the talk. Harris, The A.I. Dilemma, t=1:04:54 **[GT-label]**: "we know that leaving this presentation ... there's going to be this weird snap back effect." Raskin follows with "just be really kind with yourselves", t=1:05:24 **[GT-label]** (Raskin, not Harris).
- He will not promise a good ending. Harris, Wisdom, t=29:49 **[solo]**: "I can't tell you that there's a perfect path to get there, that it's all going to be okay." Harris, Presencing, t=7:02: "I cannot offer your group and or anyone in the world comfort that there is simply this path out of this." And t=9:22: "I orient every day as if there is a way through this."
- Seeing clearly as the precondition: "clarity creates agency" (TED 2025, `harris.md` row 12).

### 7. What he asks the listener to do

Always a specific role and a specific question, never a general "be aware".

- Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "What else that should be happening that's not happening needs to happen? And how do we help close that gap? And we don't know the answer to that question."
- Harris, Wisdom, t=29:32 **[solo]**: "what is the gap between what needs to happen and what is happening and how can we help close the gaps ... anyone in this room that has influence ... please use your power to do so."
- Harris, AI for Good 2024, t=17:33 to 19:49 **[solo]**: a list of "what if" proposals (equal spending on safety and capabilities, whistleblower protection, developer liability, using AI to upgrade governance), then: "this is just a small sample, this is not the solution to all the problems that I've laid out."
- Raskin **[CHT-PDF]**, the call: "for every technologist, every regulator, every institution lead out there, this is the call. How do we upgrade our 19th-century laws, our 19th-century institutions for the 21st century?"
- Harris, Presencing 2026, t=12:26: "the answer to this big AI crisis that we're in has to be nothing less than something we call the human movement"; closing "go see the AI doc ... I don't make any money when people see it."

## How he builds an explanation

### Structure

- **Opens with a personal image or a stake, not a definition.** The A.I. Dilemma opens with his Oppenheimer call, a first-person scene. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "The feeling that I've had personally just to share is, it's like it's 1944 and you get a call from Robert Oppenheimer inside this thing called the Manhattan Project. You have no idea what that is. And he says, the world is about to change in a fundamental way, except it's not being deployed in a safe and responsible way ... And will you help from the outside?" He then softens the metaphor: "when I say Oppenheimer, I mean more of a metaphor of a large number of people who are concerned about this." Wisdom opens on a breath and the doomer label (section 4). AI for Good opens by stating the benefit side and his premise: "you're going to hear some maybe some more critical or negative things about the risks of AI in this presentation, but the premise of this is we're all in this room because we care about which direction the future goes" (t=0:55).
- **Names the structure before the content.** Raskin **[CHT-PDF]**: "This just names the structure of the problem. So first, when you invent a new technology, you uncover a new class of responsibility ... And then two, if that technology confers power, it will start a race. And if you do not coordinate the race will end in tragedy." Three rules, each with a short dated example (right to be forgotten, Brandeis and privacy).
- **Repeating template, used in all three talks:** (1) the story we tell is true; (2) symptoms underneath; (3) the incentive underneath those; (4) "if you show me the incentive I will show you the outcome"; (5) first contact (curation AI, social media) as proof we lost; (6) second contact (generative AI) with the same incentive; (7) what could change. AI for Good, t=5:12: "beneath those in those symptoms were incentives."
- **Turn.** The hinge is a stated pause ("take a breath") followed by "we can still choose". Harris, Wisdom, t=29:12: "while the things I've shared with you today might seem really depressing, I want to point our attention at how quickly I think people are waking up."
- **Close.** The A.I. Dilemma returns to its opening ("we'll sort of return to the very beginning of this presentation", **[GT-label]**, t=1:04:03, Raskin), then predicts the audience's own snap-back, then thanks and takes questions. Later talks close on a concrete list plus an honest limit (AI for Good t=19:49, Wisdom t=29:49).

### Analogies and metaphors

Mostly one family: physical-world safety and old technology shocks. Each is a single, familiar object.

- Plane. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "That would be like if you're about to get on a plane and 50% of the engineers who make the plane say, well, if you get on this plane, there's a 10% chance that everybody goes down. Would you get on that plane?" Reused: "We don't want a world where five people at five companies onboard humanity onto the AI plane without figuring out what future we actually want." (Harris **[CHT-PDF]**.)
- Locks. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "it's as if all these locks that are locking all the doors in our society, we just unlocked all those locks."
- Hockey. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "We want to, like Wayne Gretzky, sort of skate to where the puck's going to be."
- Nuclear testing. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "I think of this public deployment of AI as above ground testing of AI. We don't need to do that." And: "It's like calculus, if calculus is available to everyone."
- Eye blind spot for exponentials. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "if you look kind of like right here in your eye, there's literally a blind spot because your eye has a nerve ending that won't let you see what's right there. And we have a blind spot paradigmatically with exponential curves."
- Car steering and brakes. Harris, AI for Good, t=1:44 **[solo]**: "it's like you're going faster and faster in a car but your steering wheel and your brakes have to get more and more precise as the complexity is going up."
- King with a smartphone. Harris, AI for Good, t=16:17 **[solo]**: "if you imagine 20th century technology crashing down on 16th century governance and the king is sitting there and suddenly smartphones and social media and Wi-Fi and radio and television all dumped on his society at the same time and he assembles his advisers, he doesn't have the governance tools to deal with those problems."
- Tower or ladder of benefits, as above (skyscraper 2024, ladder 2023, tower in `harris.md` row 17).
- Borrowed one-liners, credited: E.O. Wilson ("Paleolithic brains, medieval institutions, and godlike technology", AI for Good t=2:02; Wisdom t=23:24; Presencing t=1:42); Charlie Munger on incentives; Yuval Harari, quoted by Raskin **[CHT-PDF]**, "what nukes are to the physical world, AI is to the virtual and symbolic world."
- Raskin's contribution to the image bank: "teach an AI to fish and it'll teach itself biology, chemistry, oceanography, evolutionary theory and then fish all the fish to extinction." (Raskin **[CHT-PDF]**.)

### Concrete versus abstract

- A number plus a human-scale picture. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "This is a graph of how long it took Facebook to reach a hundred million users. It took them four and a half years. It took Instagram two and a half years. It took ChatGPT two months."
- A scene the listener enters. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "I want you to really imagine you're a kid, you're 13 years old, you've got your friends who you're chatting with ... Your other friends stop talking to you after 10:00 PM at night. But there's this AI that will always talk to you." (Raskin then reads the screenshot conversation.)
- One image reused across talks: the "Google soup" melting-logo pun (Wisdom t=13:02; The A.I. Dilemma t=18:42). He uses it to argue against "just a stochastic parrot".
- Abstraction is always followed by an example within a sentence or two: "the incentive" is followed by Munger, then the TikTok beautification filter that "gave me lip fillers".

### Rhythm, second person, questions, repetition, register

- Short declarative runs after a long spoken sentence. "And these things are all true. These are real benefits. These are real things that are going to happen." (Harris **[CHT-PDF]**.) Three parallel sentences, each a little more specific.
- Direct questions the listener would ask, answered at once. Harris, AI for Good, t=4:04 **[solo]**: "how did first contact go? Well, I would say we lost. How did we lose? How did we lose?" Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "And I know that some people might be saying, but hold on a second, what about China? If we slow down public deployment of AIs, aren't we just going to lose to China?"
- Pre-empts the listener's objection in the second person. Harris, AI for Good, t=13:06 **[solo]**: "you're probably thinking that I'm here to tell you AI is going to be used to cancel people and that's the main thing we should be concerned about, and the answer is no, this is just one example of thousands." And t=17:18: "you're probably thinking that sounds weird coming from him, sounds like a techno optimist."
- Speaks to the listener's state. Harris **[CHT-PDF]**: "try not to think about, am I scared about this example yet? You might be like, I'm not actually scared of that example. It's going to keep going at an exponential curve."
- Self-implicating admissions: "my college friends, like good human beings" who built the platforms (Wisdom, t=0:42); "I used to not be freaking out" (Wisdom, t=7:21).
- Register: spoken, plain, hedged ("sort of", "you know"), occasionally vivid and informal ("creepy", "monster", "alien"). The technical term is introduced and then translated ("paradigmatically" repeatedly). This is the weakest habit for a written essay; the jargon needs translating or cutting.
- A "name it" habit: "just to name it explicitly" (Raskin), "I just want to name" (Raskin, t=38:32), "to name a little bit". He treats naming a feeling as a step toward handling it.

## Lessons for a calm, plain-language essay

1. **State the benefit as true, in the same register as the danger.** Harris says "these things are all true" and names benefits he personally wants. Give each side concrete content and equal sentence length; do not use the benefit as a throat-clearing sentence before "but".
2. **Prove "same capability" with a matched pair, not an assertion.** Pairs like image art and abuse imagery, tutor and weapons tutor, drug discovery flipped. Use one pair per essay; choose the one that does not need a disturbing example.
3. **Say what you are not claiming, early, and where you were wrong.** "Not ideological", "not about not building AI", "I have been skeptical". The reader who fears being recruited relaxes.
4. **Treat camps as habits of attention, not groups of people.** His own phrasing: each side "doesn't want to think about" the other's failure, and "good guys or bad guys" gets replaced by "a system". Describe what a stance looks away from; avoid naming a faction.
5. **Pace the hard part out loud.** He announces that the next part is heavy, asks for a breath, names the reader's likely feeling ("is any of this even real?"), then returns choice ("we can still choose"). A calm essay can do this with one short paragraph, not a slogan.
6. **Anchor each abstract claim in one familiar object and reuse it.** Plane, locks, steering and brakes, a tower or ladder of benefits. Pair every statistic with a human-scale picture. Check that the object does not smuggle in alarm the evidence does not support.
7. **Ask the question the reader is already asking, then answer in the next sentence.** "Would you get on that plane?", "What about China?", "you're probably thinking". Short declaratives after long ones; three parallel sentences for emphasis, not four.
